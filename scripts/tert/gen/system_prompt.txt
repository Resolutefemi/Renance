EXAMPLE BODY START
Municipal water treatment turns a raw river supply into water safe to drink, and the process runs in a fixed order that every plant operator can recite. Raw water first passes through coarse screens that hold back sticks, leaves and plastic, then through a settling basin where grit and sand sink because they are denser than water. Only after this physical cleaning does the chemical stage begin. Coagulants such as alum are dosed into the water, where they bind the fine suspended particles into larger clumps called flocs, and these flocs settle out in the clarifier over two to four hours. The order matters: screens before settling, settling before coagulation, because each stage removes a size class of dirt the next stage cannot handle.

The water is not yet potable after clarification. It still carries dissolved organic matter, taste, odour and microbes, so it flows through rapid sand filters, layers of sand and gravel that trap the remaining floc and polish the water to a low turbidity, often below one nephelometric turbidity unit. Filtered water then receives a disinfectant dose, usually chlorine, held in contact with the water for at least thirty minutes so the chemical can inactivate bacteria and viruses. Operators measure the residual chlorine, the amount left after the contact time, because a residual of about 0.2 to 0.5 milligrams per litre at the tap proves the disinfection worked and protects the distribution main.

Each stage is monitored with numbers, not guesses. Turbidity is checked hourly, the chlorine residual several times a day, and a laboratory runs bacteriological tests on samples drawn from different points of the network. In {S}, a student who can trace one drop of water from intake to tap, naming the unit operations in order and the quantity each one controls, understands the whole plant. That is the working standard for this topic: process first, mechanism second, numbers third, and the classic operating errors always tied back to the stage where they occur.
